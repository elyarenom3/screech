Part 1 20 minutes of reading

Understand the robot

Sesame is a four-legged walking robot about the size of a bar of soap. Each leg has two small motors called servos. One swings the leg forward and back. The other raises and lowers it. A small computer board, the Lolin S2 Mini, tells all eight servos where to point, many times each second. A small screen on top shows an animated face. You control the robot from a phone over Wi-Fi.

Why?
One servo turns around one axis only. A walking leg has to swing forward and also lift, so it needs two servos. The servos have no program of their own: each moves to whatever angle the board's signal asks for, refreshed about 50 times a second.

The names used everywhere

The project gives every joint a short name. R means the robot's right side and L its left. The four hip joints are R1, R2, L1 and L2. The four legs are R3, R4, L3 and L4. Each servo also has a number from S0 to S7, which is the order the program uses. You will meet these names on the printed parts, on the wiring and on the phone screen.

Why?
The joint names say where a part sits on the body. The S numbers say the order the program stores the servos in, which is also the order of the columns on the header block. The table links the two.

How the electricity flows

Three kinds of thing travel along the wires. Power is 5 volts, carried on red wire. Ground is the return path that completes every circuit, carried on black or brown wire. Signals are the instructions, carried on thin wires. Every part needs power and ground. Each servo also needs one signal wire of its own.

Why?
Electricity only flows round a complete loop: out along the power wire, through the part, and back along ground. Each signal wire tells one servo its angle. A shared signal would make every servo copy the same angle.

Words you will meet

Servo
A motor that turns to the angle you ask for, from 0° to 180°, and holds it.
Why?
Inside are a small motor, gears and a sensor that reads the shaft's angle. The servo compares that angle with the one it is asked for and drives the motor until they match. That is how it holds a position.
Horn
The small plastic arm that pushes onto a servo's output shaft.
Why?
The shaft is tiny. The horn gives a larger piece to fix a printed part to.
Spline
The ring of fine teeth on the shaft and inside the horn. The teeth give fixed positions about 17° apart.
Why?
The teeth lock the horn to the shaft so it cannot slip when the leg pushes. They also mean a horn can only sit in fixed positions, which is why fitting is done in whole teeth.
S2 Mini, controller, ESP32
Three names for the robot's computer board.
Why?
ESP32 is the family of chips. The S2 Mini is a small board built around one of them. Controller describes the job it does.
Pin, hole, GPIO
A numbered connection point on the controller. A wire is soldered into each one you use.
Why?
GPIO stands for general purpose input and output. The program decides what each pin does, so the number in the program must match the hole the wire is soldered into.
Firmware, program, sketch
The software that runs on the controller.
Why?
Firmware is software stored on the board itself. It starts by itself whenever the board gets power. Sketch is the Arduino word for a program.
Upload, flash
Copying the program onto the controller through the USB cable.
Why?
The board's memory is a type called flash memory, which is where the second name comes from.
OLED
The small screen. This handbook calls it the display.
Why?
Each tiny dot makes its own light, so the screen needs no backlight.
SDA and SCL
The two signal wires of the display: data and clock.
Why?
Together they form a link called I2C. SCL is a steady on and off pulse that sets the timing. SDA sends one bit of data on each pulse.
Rail
A shared wire that feeds many parts. The robot has a 5 V rail and a ground rail.
Why?
One shared wire feeding all eight servos saves running eight separate pairs of wires from the power supply.
GND
Ground. All ground points in the robot are joined together.
Why?
Voltage is always measured between two points. Ground is the shared reference point that every part measures from, so all grounds must be joined.
VBUS
The S2 Mini's 5 V pin. 3V3 is its 3.3 volt output pin.
Why?
VBUS is the name USB gives to its 5 V line. 3V3 comes from a small regulator on the board that lowers 5 V to 3.3 V for the chip.
Buck converter
A small board that lowers the battery's 7.4 V to a steady 5 V.
Why?
Fed 7.4 V directly, the servos and the S2 Mini could be damaged. A buck converter switches the battery on and off many thousands of times a second and smooths the result into a steady, lower voltage, with little energy lost as heat.
Solder
Soft metal wire that melts at the iron's tip and joins two metal parts as it cools.
Why?
Solder melts at a much lower temperature than copper, so it flows into a joint and grips both metals without melting them.
Flux
A liquid that helps molten solder flow and stick. The solder wire has some inside. The flux pen adds more.
Why?
Metal grows a thin skin of oxide in the air, and solder slides off that skin. Flux clears the skin as it heats, so the solder can bond.
Tinning
Coating a wire end, a pin or the iron tip with a thin layer of solder.
Why?
A tinned surface already holds solder, so the final joint needs less heat and less time.
Heat-shrink
Plastic tube that shrinks tightly around a joint when heated, to insulate it.
Why?
Bare metal can touch other metal and cause a short. The sleeve covers the joint and also supports the wire where it bends.
AWG
A wire thickness scale. A larger number means a thinner wire. 22 AWG carries power. 30 AWG carries signals.
Why?
The number comes from how many times the wire is pulled through smaller and smaller holes to make it. More pulls give a higher number and a thinner wire. Thick wire carries the servos' large current without heating up. Thin wire is enough for signals and bends easily.
Polarity
Which wire is positive and which is negative.
Why?
Many parts only work, or only survive, when positive and negative go the right way round.
Short circuit
Power touching ground directly. It makes heat and can damage parts.
Why?
With nothing in the path to slow it, a battery pushes a very large current. That current turns into heat in the wires and joints within seconds.
Continuity
An unbroken metal path between two points. The multimeter beeps when it finds one.
Why?
This is how you test a joint with the power off. A beep means joined. Silence means separate.
Self-tapping screw
A screw with a coarse thread that cuts its own way into plastic.
Why?
Plastic is soft enough for the screw to form its own thread, so the printed holes are plain.
Machine screw
A screw with a fine thread for a threaded metal hole, such as a servo shaft.
Why?
The servo shaft is metal with a fine thread already cut inside it. A coarse self-tapping screw would ruin that thread.
STL, slicer, PLA, supports
An STL is a 3D shape file. A slicer turns it into printer instructions. PLA is the plastic. Supports are temporary scaffolding the printer adds under overhangs.
Why?
A printer builds one thin layer at a time and needs instructions for each layer. The slicer writes them from the shape. Supports let the printer build over empty space.

Eight safety habits

  1. Hot iron. The tip runs at about 330 °C. Rest it in its stand every time you put it down. Hold it by the handle.
    Why?
    At 330 °C the tip burns skin on contact. An iron left on the bench can roll off or burn through a cable. The stand holds it tip-up and out of the way.
  2. Moving air. Solder with a window open or a small fan carrying the smoke away from your face.
    Why?
    The smoke comes from flux heating up. It irritates eyes, nose and lungs. Moving air carries it away from your breathing.
  3. Glasses. Wear them when soldering and when clipping wire ends.
    Why?
    Boiling flux can spit tiny drops of hot solder, and clipped wire ends fly off at speed. Both can hurt an eye.
  4. Clean hands. Leaded solder: wash your hands after each session. Keep food and drink off the bench.
    Why?
    Leaded solder leaves traces of lead on your fingers. Lead is harmful when swallowed, so washing and keeping food away stops it reaching your mouth.
  5. Power off to solder. Unplug USB and the battery before soldering anything on the robot.
    Why?
    With power on, the iron's tip or a stray blob of solder can short two connections and damage parts.
  6. One power source at a time. Switch the battery off before you plug in USB.
    Why?
    USB and the battery both feed the robot's 5 V rail. With both on, the two supplies push into each other, which can damage the computer's USB port or the buck converter.
  7. Battery care. Charge it with its matching Bambu Lab charger, on a hard surface, while you are in the room. Keep its factory plug and wrapper as they are. Keep bare wire ends from touching each other.
    Why?
    Lithium batteries hold a lot of energy. The matching charger stops at the correct voltage for its cells. A hard surface lets heat escape, and being nearby lets you act early. The factory plug and wrapper are part of its protection.
  8. Damaged battery. If the battery swells, smells or gets hot, stop using it and take it to a battery recycling point.
    Why?
    Swelling, a smell or heat mean the cells are failing inside. A failing lithium battery can catch fire. Recycling points know how to handle them safely.

Printing takes many hours, so it starts first. Parts 3, 4 and 5 can all happen while the printer runs.

Why?
A printer works by itself for hours. Parts 3 to 5 need only the computer, the board, the wire and the tools, so no time is lost waiting.

Download the project files

10 minutes

You need

  • a computer with internet

Why this step. The project folder holds the printable parts and the robot's program. You download it once.

Do

  1. Open github.com/dorianborian/sesame-robot in a web browser.
    Why?
    GitHub is the website where the project's author publishes every file for the robot. Downloading from his page means you get the real, current files.
  2. Click the green Code button, then Download ZIP.
    Why?
    A ZIP file packs the whole project into one download. This route needs no GitHub account and no extra software.
  3. Open your Downloads folder and find sesame-robot-main.zip.
    Why?
    Browsers save downloads into the Downloads folder unless you have changed that setting.
  4. Unzip it. Windows: right-click the file and choose Extract All. Mac: double-click the file.
    Why?
    A ZIP file is squashed to make it smaller. Programs such as the Arduino IDE and your slicer need the real files, and extracting writes them out into a normal folder.
  5. Open the new sesame-robot-main folder and find the two highlighted folders in the diagram.
    Why?
    Only two folders matter for this build. firmware holds the robot's program. hardware holds the shapes to print. The other folders are guides and files for other versions of the robot.

Check before you move on

  • Inside firmware you can see a file named sesame-firmware-main.ino.
    Why?
    A file ending in .ino is an Arduino program. Seeing it proves the download is complete and that you are in the folder Part 3 needs.
  • Inside hardware, printing, stl you can see files ending in .stl.
    Why?
    Each .stl file is the shape of one printed part. These are the files the printer or the print service needs in this step.

Print the 11 parts

1 hour of your time, an afternoon of printing

You need

  • a 3D printer and PLA filament, or a print service
  • flush cutters
  • tape and a marker pen

Why this step. The body is 11 plastic parts. Each one has its own file.

Do

  1. Choose your route. Own printer: continue with action 2. Print service or makerspace: go to action 7.
    Why?
    The two routes need different actions. Going straight to yours saves reading printer settings that do not apply to you.
  2. Open the slicer program that came with your printer (Bambu Studio for a Bambu Lab printer).
    Why?
    A printer cannot read an STL file. The slicer cuts the shape into thin layers and works out the path the nozzle follows for each layer. The slicer from your printer's maker already knows your printer.
  3. Set the material to PLA. Set layer height 0.2 mm, wall loops 2, infill 8 to 10%, infill pattern honeycomb.
    Why?
    PLA is easy to print and stiff enough for these parts. 0.2 mm layers balance speed and detail. Two walls with a little honeycomb filling inside give strength while keeping the parts light, which matters because small servos lift the whole robot.
  4. Add the eight joint files, R1 to R4 and L1 to L4. Press the slicer's auto orient button so each part lies flat. Keep supports off. Print.
    Why?
    Auto orient lays each part on its largest flat face, which sticks well to the bed. These parts were designed to print with no supports. Supports would leave rough scars on surfaces that need to fit closely.
  5. Print the internal frame and the bottom cover the same way, with supports off.
    Why?
    The frame and the bottom cover were also designed to print flat with no supports.
  6. Print the top cover. Use the Enclosed version from the top-covers folder. Turn supports on for this part and place them as the official printing guide shows.
    Why?
    The enclosed top cover has overhangs: areas the printer would have to build over empty air. Supports hold those areas up while the plastic cools. Afterwards you snap them away. The enclosed version also covers the electronics.
  7. Print service route: upload all 11 files, choose PLA, and ask for each part to be bagged and labelled with its file name.
    Why?
    A print service prints many orders together. Bags and labels keep your 11 parts apart, and the file name tells you which part is which.
  8. Label every part as it arrives. Write R1, L3 and so on onto tape and stick it to the part. The four hip joints look alike. So do the four legs.
    Why?
    The four hip joints differ only in small mirrored details, and so do the four legs. A part fitted at the wrong corner makes the robot walk badly or jam. Labels made now save puzzling later.
  9. Clean up. Snip away support material with the flush cutters and pick off any thin strings.
    Why?
    Leftover support and stray strings stop parts sitting flush, block screw holes and can catch on moving joints.

Check before you move on

  • You have 11 parts and each one carries a label.
    Why?
    Eleven is the full set: eight joints, the frame, the bottom cover and the top cover. A missing part is quick to reprint now and slow to discover halfway through.
  • Each part lies flat on the table without rocking.
    Why?
    A part that rocks has bent as it cooled. Bent parts leave gaps and put strain on screws and servos.
  • Drive one M2 self-tapping screw two turns into a screw hole in the frame, then back it out. It cuts in with firm, steady resistance.
    Why?
    This proves the plastic is thick enough to hold a screw. If the screw spins freely, the walls are too thin or the hole too large, and that part needs printing again with more walls.

Unpack, identify and charge

30 minutes

You need

  • everything you bought
  • the silicone mat

Why this step. Finding a missing or wrong part now takes a few minutes. Finding it halfway through the build takes days of waiting.

Do

  1. Lay every part on the mat and match it to its drawing in the diagram.
    Why?
    Matching each part to its drawing teaches you its name and shape before any step asks for it.
  2. Compare the parts with the table below.
    Why?
    The table lists how many of each part you need. Counting now finds a missing item while there is still time to order it.
  3. Check that the servo label or listing says 180 degree.
    Why?
    A 180 degree servo turns to the angle you ask for and holds it there. A 360 degree servo looks the same but spins round and round and cannot hold an angle. The robot needs the 180 degree kind to hold its poses.
  4. Open one servo bag. Find the single-arm horn and the small screws. Keep the other bags closed for now.
    Why?
    You need to recognise a horn and its screws for Steps 18 and 19. Keeping the other bags closed keeps their tiny screws together.
  5. Read the print on the controller board: it says S2 mini.
    Why?
    Several boards look alike. The pin numbers in this handbook match the Lolin S2 Mini only.
  6. Read the display's four pin labels: GND, VCC (or VDD), SCL (or SCK) and SDA.
    Why?
    Makers put the display's pins in different orders. Reading your own labels now means you wire Step 17 to match your own display.
  7. Start charging the battery. Plug the battery into its charger. Plug the charger into a computer USB port or a phone charger. A red light means charging. The light goes out when the battery is full, after about two hours.
    Why?
    A full battery is needed in Steps 12 and 26. Charging takes about two hours, so it can run while you do other things. The light shows the state without any tools.

Computer and controller

You load the robot's program onto the S2 Mini before any soldering. That proves the board, the cable and the computer all work while the board is still easy to replace.

Install the Arduino IDE and ESP32 support

20 minutes

You need

  • a computer with internet

Why this step. The Arduino IDE is the free program that sends the robot's program to the S2 Mini. When first installed it knows only Arduino boards, so you add support for the ESP32 family.

Do

  1. Go to arduino.cc/en/software. Download Arduino IDE 2 for your computer, install it with the default options and open it.
    Why?
    The Arduino IDE turns the program's text into instructions the chip understands, then sends them down the USB cable. The default options also install the USB drivers your computer needs to talk to the board.
  2. Open File, then Preferences. On a Mac open Arduino IDE, then Settings.
    Why?
    The setting you need next lives in this window.
  3. Find the box named Additional boards manager URLs. Paste this address into it and click OK:
    https://raw.githubusercontent.com/espressif/arduino-esp32/gh-pages/package_esp32_index.json
    Why?
    This address points to a list published by Espressif, the company that makes the ESP32 chip. The list tells the IDE where to download support for ESP32 boards. Until it is added, the search in the next action finds nothing.
  4. Click the Boards Manager icon in the left bar, number 1 in the diagram.
    Why?
    The Boards Manager installs support for whole families of boards. Each family needs its own translator, called a compiler, and its own upload tool.
  5. Type esp32 in the search box. Find the entry esp32 by Espressif Systems and click Install. The download is large. Allow 5 to 15 minutes.
    Why?
    This package holds the compiler for ESP32 chips, the upload tool and the settings for hundreds of boards, the S2 Mini among them. That is why the download is large.

Check before you move on

  • Open Tools, then Board. A group named esp32 appears and its list contains LOLIN S2 Mini.
    Why?
    Seeing LOLIN S2 Mini in the list proves the package installed completely. Step 7 selects this exact board.
5

Install three libraries

10 minutes

You need

  • the Arduino IDE

Why this step. Libraries are ready-made code that the robot's program relies on. One drives the servos. Two drive the display.

Do

  1. Click the Library Manager icon in the left bar, number 2 in the Step 4 diagram.
    Why?
    Libraries installed through the Library Manager are stored where the IDE looks for them when it builds the program.
  2. Type ESP32Servo in the search box. In the result named exactly ESP32Servo, open the version box and choose 3.0.9. Then click Install.
    Why?
    This library turns an instruction such as "move to 90°" into the timed electrical pulses a servo understands. The version matters because of the fault described in the note below.
  3. Type Adafruit SSD1306. Click Install on the result with that exact name. A window offers to install other libraries it needs. Choose Install all.
    Why?
    SSD1306 is the name of the chip inside the display. This library speaks to that chip. It relies on other libraries to draw shapes and text, and Install all fetches those at the same time.
  4. Type Adafruit GFX and confirm that Adafruit GFX Library shows as installed.
    Why?
    The program uses both display libraries. Confirming now saves a failed build in Step 7.

Why version 3.0.9

Newer versions of ESP32Servo have a fault where moving one servo can move others. The project asks for 3.0.9. If the IDE later offers library updates, keep ESP32Servo at 3.0.9.

Check before you move on

  • ESP32Servo shows version 3.0.9 as installed.
    Why?

Set up the soldering station

15 minutes

You need

  • soldering iron, stand and brass wool
  • solder, flux pen and solder wick
  • silicone mat and helping hands
  • safety glasses
  • a small fan or an open window

Why this step. A fixed layout keeps the hot iron in one known place.

Do

  1. Choose a table near a mains socket and a window. Clear it.
    Why?
    The iron's cable must reach without stretching across the bench. The window clears smoke. A clear table leaves nothing to knock over or melt.
  2. Lay the mat. Put the iron stand on the side of your writing hand, with the brass wool beside it.
    Why?
    You pick the iron up and put it down dozens of times. On the side of your writing hand the reach is short, and the hot tip stays clear of your other arm.
  3. Put the solder, flux pen, wick, cutters, stripper and tweezers on the other side.
    Why?
    Your other hand holds the solder and the small tools, so they sit on that side. It also keeps their cables and wires away from the hot stand.
  4. Stand the fan at a back corner so the air moves across the work and away from you. Open the window.
    Why?
    The smoke is mostly flux turning to vapour. It irritates eyes and lungs. A gentle flow of air carries it away from your face. A strong blast straight at the joint would cool it.
  5. Put on the glasses. Tie back long hair and loose sleeves.
    Why?
    Boiling flux can spit tiny drops of hot solder, and clipped wire ends fly off. Loose hair and sleeves can brush the hot tip or drag the iron off its stand.
  6. Switch the iron on and set it to 330 °C. Wait until it reaches temperature.
    Why?
    Solder melts at about 183 to 220 °C depending on its type. At 330 °C the iron heats a joint in about two seconds. Much hotter burns the flux away before it can work and can lift the thin copper pads off a board.
  7. Tin the tip. Touch solder to the tip until a thin shiny coat covers it, then wipe the tip once through the brass wool. Repeat this whenever the tip looks dull.
    Why?
    A thin coat of melted solder carries heat from the tip into the joint far better than dry metal touching at a single point. It also protects the tip from the air, which would darken it. Brass wool scrapes off old, dirty solder without cooling the tip the way a wet sponge does.
  8. When you pause, leave a little solder on the tip and rest the iron in its stand. Switch it off when you leave the table.
    Why?
    The coat of solder protects the tip while it waits. Switching off when you leave makes the tip last longer and removes a burn risk.

Check before you move on

  • The tip is shiny silver.
    Why?
    A shiny tip passes heat well. A dull or dark tip has a thin skin that solder slides off, and joints come out poor.
  • The iron rests in its stand between uses.
    Why?
    An iron laid on the table can roll, burn the mat or a cable, or be picked up by the hot end.
  • You can feel air moving across the mat.
    Why?
    Moving air proves the smoke will be carried away from you.

Practise soldering pins

30 minutes

You need

  • the spare protoboard
  • 12 pins from the header strip
  • iron, solder, solder wick
  • tape

Why this step. Every joint in this robot uses the same four moves. Twelve practice joints make the real ones routine.

Do

  1. Snap a strip of 6 pins off the header strip. Grip it with the flush cutters at a groove and bend. Snap off a second strip of 6.
    Why?
    The plastic bar has a thin groove between each pair of pins. Bending at a groove breaks the bar cleanly and leaves both pieces usable.
  2. Push the short pins of both strips through holes near one edge of the spare protoboard. The black plastic bar sits on top.
    Why?
    Header pins have a short end for the board and a long end for plugs. The plastic bar holds the pins straight and evenly spaced.
  3. Turn the board over so the short pin tips point up. Hold the strips flat with tape or the helping hands.
    Why?
    You solder on the copper side, where the short pins come through. Tape holds the strips still so they cannot tilt or drop out while you work.
  4. Touch the iron tip so it presses on the copper pad and the pin together. Count two seconds.
    Why?
    Solder only sticks to metal that is hot enough to melt it. The tip must touch the pad and the pin together so both heat up.
  5. Touch the solder to the meeting point of pad and pin, on the side away from the tip. Feed in 2 to 3 mm. It melts and flows around the pin.
    Why?
    If the solder melts there, the joint itself is hot enough. Melted solder flows towards heat, so it runs around the pin and over the pad. Solder melted on the tip alone would land on cold metal and fail to bond.
  6. Take the solder away first. Then take the iron away. Keep the board still for three seconds.
    Why?
    If the iron came away first, the solder wire would freeze into the joint. Solder takes a moment to set, and movement while it sets leaves a dull, cracked joint that can lose contact.
  7. Compare your joint with the bottom row of the diagram. A good joint is a small shiny cone.
    Why?
    A cone shape shows the solder bonded to the pin and the pad. A round ball means one of them was too cold. A flat puddle means too much heat or too little solder.
  8. Solder all 12 pins. Wipe and tin the tip again every few joints.
    Why?
    Repetition makes the moves automatic. The tip loses its coat as you work, and a dull tip passes heat badly.
  9. Practise one repair. Join two neighbouring pins with extra solder on purpose. Lay the solder wick over the bridge, press the iron on top of the wick for two seconds, and lift both away together. The wick absorbs the extra solder.
    Why?
    A bridge between two pins is a common beginner fault. Solder wick is a braid of fine copper wires coated in flux. When heated it pulls the melted solder into its strands and away from the pins.

Check before you move on

  • You have 12 cones. Each one covers its pad and climbs the pin.
    Why?
    A cone that covers the pad and climbs the pin proves the solder bonded to both.
  • Each pin stands separate from its neighbours.
    Why?
    Two pins joined by solder would connect two connections that must stay apart.

Practise wires and heat-shrink

30 minutes

You need

  • 22 AWG and 30 AWG wire
  • wire stripper
  • heat-shrink tubing
  • heat gun or lighter
  • the spare protoboard

Why this step. The robot has about 35 wire ends. Each one is stripped, twisted, tinned and soldered in the same way.

Do

  1. Cut 8 cm of 22 AWG wire. Place 4 mm of its end in the stripper notch marked 22, squeeze, and pull the cover off.
    Why?
    Each notch is sized for one wire thickness. The right notch cuts through the plastic cover and stops at the copper. A smaller notch would cut strands and weaken the wire. 4 mm is enough bare wire for a joint and leaves no extra to touch neighbours.
  2. Twist the bare strands between finger and thumb into one neat bundle.
    Why?
    Loose strands splay out, miss the hole and can touch other pins. A twisted bundle stays together and slides through a hole.
  3. Tin it. Hold the iron under the bare end and touch solder to the top. The solder soaks into the strands in about one second.
    Why?
    Heat rises up through the strands, so the solder on top is drawn down into the whole bundle. A tinned end keeps its shape and joins quickly later, because solder is already there.
  4. Repeat with 30 AWG wire and the notch marked 30. This wire is fine. Squeeze gently and strip 3 mm.
    Why?
    Thin wire has fewer, finer strands. A hard squeeze nicks them, and a nicked strand snaps after a few bends. Signal wires need only a short bare end.
  5. Join two wires. Slide a 15 mm piece of heat-shrink onto one wire and push it well back from the end. Lay the two tinned ends side by side, overlapping. Touch the iron to them until the solder on both melts into one.
    Why?
    After the two wires are joined, there is no free end left to slide the sleeve over. Pushing it well back stops the iron's heat shrinking it too early.
  6. Let the joint cool. Slide the heat-shrink over it. Warm it with the heat gun, or with the side of a lighter flame held 2 cm away, until it grips tightly.
    Why?
    Heat-shrink tightens to about half its width when warm. It covers the bare joint so it cannot touch other metal. The side of a flame is hot enough. The tip of the flame would scorch it.
  7. Solder a wire to a board. Push a tinned end through a protoboard hole, solder it as you soldered a pin, and snip the spare length flush.
    Why?
    You will make this joint many times in the robot. A leftover end can bend over and touch a neighbouring joint, so it is cut level with the solder.
  8. Tug each joint gently. A good joint holds.
    Why?
    A gentle pull finds a joint where the solder failed to bond. Finding it now is easy. Finding it inside the closed robot is hard.

Check before you move on

  • You can strip 30 AWG wire with all its strands intact.
    Why?
    Every strand carries part of the current and adds strength. A wire that has lost strands can break inside its cover later.
  • Your sleeved joint is tight and shows no bare metal.
    Why?
    A loose sleeve can slide off and leave bare metal showing.

Learn two multimeter settings

15 minutes

You need

  • multimeter
  • the charged battery
  • the XH pigtail lead
  • a spare piece of wire

Why this step. The meter answers two questions for the rest of the build. Are these two points joined? How many volts are here?

Safety

Hold the pigtail's two bare ends well apart for the whole time the battery is clipped on. If they touch, the battery's full current flows through them.

Why?
These are bare wires straight from the battery, with nothing to limit the current. If they touch, a very large current flows at once: wires heat up within seconds, sparks fly and the battery can be damaged.

Do

  1. Push the black probe lead into the socket marked COM and the red lead into the socket marked V.
    Why?
    COM is the shared socket used for every measurement. The V socket is for measuring volts and checking continuity. The third socket, marked A, is for measuring current, and inside the meter it is close to a plain wire. A probe left in A and touched across a battery makes a short circuit.
  2. Turn the dial to the continuity setting, marked with a sound symbol. Touch the probe tips together. The meter beeps.
    Why?
    On the continuity setting the meter pushes a tiny current out of one probe and checks whether it comes back through the other. If metal joins the two tips, the current flows round and the meter beeps. Touching the tips together proves the setting and both leads work. The meter supplies its own small current, so anything you test this way must have its power off.
  3. Touch one probe to each bare end of a spare wire. It beeps: the wire is one unbroken path. Lift one probe. The beep stops.
    Why?
    This is the meter's way of asking "are these two points joined?". A beep means one unbroken metal path. Silence means a gap. Every continuity check in this handbook uses this idea.
  4. Turn the dial to DC voltage: the V with a straight line. On a meter with numbered ranges choose 20.
    Why?
    A battery gives direct current, DC, which always flows the same way. The wavy symbol is for mains electricity, AC. On a meter with numbered ranges you choose the smallest range above what you expect. The battery gives up to 8.4 V, so 20 is the right range. The 2 range would overflow and show 1 or OL.
  5. Click the XH pigtail onto the battery's plug. It fits one way round.
    Why?
    The pigtail is a plug that fits the battery, with two bare wire ends on the other side. It turns the battery's plug into wires you can measure and later solder. The plug is shaped to fit one way only.
  6. Touch the red probe to the bare end of the pigtail's red wire and the black probe to the bare end of its black wire.
    Why?
    Voltage is a difference between two points. The meter shows how much higher the point under the red probe is compared with the point under the black probe.
  7. Read the display. A value from 7 to 8.4 with no minus sign means the colours are true: red is positive.
    Why?
    This battery holds two lithium cells joined end to end. Each cell gives 3.5 to 4.2 V, so together they give 7 to 8.4 V. No minus sign means the red probe sits on the positive wire.
  8. If the value shows a minus sign, this pigtail's colours are reversed. Wrap red tape around the wire that the red probe touches when the reading has no minus sign. Wrap black tape around the other wire. Follow the tape for the rest of the build.
    Why?
    Some pigtails are made with their colours swapped. A minus sign means the probes are on the opposite wires to what the colours say. Tape records the truth for every later step, because power connected backwards can destroy parts.
  9. Unclip the pigtail from the battery. Turn the meter off.
    Why?
    With the battery unclipped, the bare ends carry no power. Turning the meter off saves its own battery.

Check before you move on

  • You know which pigtail wire is positive, by its colour or by your tape.
    Why?
    Every power connection later depends on this. A battery connected backwards can damage the buck converter, the S2 Mini and the servos all at once.
  • The pigtail is unclipped from the battery.
    Why?
    With the battery clipped on, the bare ends are live and could short if they touched.

Build the servo header block

You need

  • pin header strip
  • craft knife and metal ruler
  • marker pen
  • iron and solder
  • the printed internal frame

Why this step. The eight servo plugs push onto this block. It gives every servo its three connections: signal, 5 V and ground.

Do

  1. Look at the electronics bay in your printed internal frame and at the official photo of the electronics in place. The header block shares that bay with the S2 Mini.
    Why?
    The block shares a small bay with the S2 Mini. Seeing the space first helps you place the block and leave room for wires.
  2. Mark out a piece of protoboard 10 holes long and 5 holes wide, about 26 by 13 mm.
    Why?
    Eight columns of pins need eight holes, plus a spare column at each end. Three rows of pins need three holes, plus a spare row on each side. The spare holes keep the pins away from the cut edges.
  3. Cut it out. Lay the metal ruler along the row of holes on the cut line. Score along it with the craft knife six times. Turn the board over and score the same line six times. Hold the line over a table edge and snap. Repeat for the second cut. Cut on a surface you can scratch, with the blade moving away from your hand.
    Why?
    Protoboard is a stiff, brittle sheet. Scoring both sides makes a weak line, and the board breaks cleanly along it. Cutting along a row of holes helps, because the holes already weaken that line. A blade moving away from your hand cannot slip into it.
  4. Snap three strips of 8 pins from the header strip.
    Why?
    Each strip becomes one row: signal, power or ground, for all eight servos.
  5. Push the three strips into the middle three rows, side by side, with one empty column of holes at each end. Short pins go through the board. Black bars sit on top.
    Why?
    Short pins go through the board so the long ends stand up for the servo plugs. Cut edges can be rough or cracked, so the spare holes keep every pin on solid board.
  6. Tape the strips down, turn the board over, and solder the two end pins of each strip. Check that all three strips sit flat and upright. If one leans, reheat an end joint and press the strip flat.
    Why?
    Two joints per strip hold it in place while it can still be straightened. Once all the pins are soldered, a leaning strip is very hard to correct.
  7. Solder the remaining pins: 24 joints in total.
    Why?
    Each pin needs its own joint so it makes contact with the rail wire added in the next step.
  8. Turn the board pins-up. With the marker write S beside one outer row, + beside the middle row and G beside the other outer row. Number the columns 0 to 7.
    Why?
    S marks signal, + marks 5 V and G marks ground. Column numbers 0 to 7 match the servo numbers S0 to S7. Every later step refers to these labels.

  • Continuity setting: touch any two neighbouring pins. The meter stays silent for every pair.
    Why?

Add the 5 V rail and the ground rail

30 minutes

You need

  • 22 AWG wire, red and black
  • wire stripper
  • iron and solder
  • multimeter

Why this step. All eight servos share one 5 V supply and one ground. A bare wire soldered along a whole row links its eight pins into one rail.

Do

  1. Flip the board over, top edge towards you, as in the diagram. The G row is now the far row. The + row is in the middle.
    Why?
    The rails go on the underside, where the pin joints are. Turning the board to match the diagram keeps the rows the right way round.
  2. Cut 10 cm of red 22 AWG wire. Strip 24 mm from one end in three bites of 8 mm. Twist the strands.
    Why?
    Strippers grip only a short length of plastic. Pulling 24 mm off in a single pull takes a lot of force, because the plastic grips the strands along its whole length. The wire can stretch, strands can tear, or the plastic can bunch up and jam. Three short pulls of 8 mm each need little force and leave the strands straight and whole.
  3. Lay the bare section along the eight joints of the + row. The coloured part leaves the board at the column 0 end.
    Why?
    One bare wire touching all eight joints connects the eight + pins together. That shared wire is the rail. The coloured, insulated part at column 0 becomes the tail that later runs to the S2 Mini.
  4. Solder the bare wire to the column 0 joint, then the column 7 joint, then the six between. Add a little solder at each.
    Why?
    Fixing both ends first holds the wire straight and tight along the row. Each joint in between then only needs a moment of heat to flow onto it.
  5. Repeat with 10 cm of black 22 AWG wire along the G row.
    Why?
    The ground row needs its own rail, built the same way. Black marks ground so you can tell the two rails apart by eye.
  6. Leave the S row as it is. Its eight joints stay separate.
    Why?
    Each servo needs its own signal. Joining the S pins would make all eight servos follow the same instruction.
  7. Snip off any bare wire that sticks out beyond column 7.
    Why?
    A bare end sticking out past the board could touch the other rail or the S2 Mini and cause a short.

Check before you move on

  • + to +: the + pin of column 0 to the + pin of column 7 beeps.
    Why?
    This proves the rail joins column 0 to column 7, so every + pin will receive power.
  • G to G: the G pin of column 0 to the G pin of column 7 beeps.
    Why?
    This proves the ground rail joins all eight G pins.
  • + to G: any + pin to any G pin stays silent. This check protects every part in the robot.
    Why?
    If + and G were joined, the battery would push a huge current straight through that spot the moment power arrived. Wires would heat up and the buck converter or the battery could be damaged.
  • S to rails: each S pin to the + row and to the G row stays silent.
    Why?

Why this step. Each servo listens on its own signal wire. These eight wires will run from the S row to the S2 Mini.

Do

Why this step. This step gives each servo its own control pin and brings 5 V and ground to the controller.

Do

    Why?

Wire the display

40 minutes

You need

  • the display
  • 30 AWG wire in four colours
  • thin heat-shrink
  • iron and solder
  • multimeter

Why this step. The display needs four wires: power, ground and two signal wires.

Do

  1. Read the four labels printed beside the display's pins. A common order is GND, VCC, SCL, SDA. Some displays swap the first two. Go by the labels on your own display.
    Why?
    Makers put GND and VCC in different places. Wiring by your own labels keeps power going into the right pin.
  2. Cut four pieces of 30 AWG wire, each 10 cm long, in four colours. Suggested: black for GND, red for VCC, yellow for SCL, blue for SDA. Write your colours down.
    Why?
    Colours that match each wire's job make checking easy. 10 cm leaves slack so the top cover can open while the display sits in it.
  3. Strip 3 mm from both ends of all four wires and tin them.
    Why?
    A short bare end avoids touching neighbours. Tinning makes the joint quick.
  4. Slide a 12 mm piece of thin heat-shrink onto each wire and push it well away from the end.
    Why?
    The display's pins sit very close together. A sleeve on each joint stops neighbours touching when the wires bend into the cover. It goes on now, because once the far end is soldered, the sleeve has no free end to slide on from.
  5. Tin the display's four pins. Solder each wire along its pin: lay the tinned wire end against the tinned pin and touch the iron to both for one second.
    Why?
    A wire laid along a pin touches over a long length, which makes a strong joint. With both parts already tinned, one second of heat joins them, so the display is not overheated.
  6. When the joints are cool, slide each heat-shrink sleeve over its pin and shrink it. Each pin ends up insulated from its neighbours.
    Why?
    Insulated pins cannot touch each other or the screws that hold the display.
  7. Solder the other ends into the S2 Mini from the top side. GND wire into the second GND hole. VCC wire into 3V3. SCL wire into 35. SDA wire into 33.
    Why?
    GND and 3V3 power the display. Pins 33 and 35 are the two pins the program uses to talk to the display, set by the lines you checked in Step 6. SDA carries the data. SCL carries a steady timing pulse that tells the display when to read each piece of data.
  8. If your display arrived with its pin strip loose in the bag, solder the four wires straight into the display's four holes.
    Why?
    The holes take a wire directly. Soldering straight in gives a lower, neater joint and saves fitting the pin strip.

Display power

This display runs on 3.3 V or 5 V. This handbook uses the S2 Mini's 3V3 pin, which matches the level of its signal pins.

Why?
Many display boards pull their two signal wires up to their own supply voltage. Powered at 5 V, they would put 5 V on the S2 Mini's signal pins, which are made for 3.3 V.

Check before you move on

  • Each display pin to its S2 Mini hole beeps: GND to GND, VCC to 3V3, SCL to 35, SDA to 33.
    Why?
    This proves each display wire reaches the right hole on the S2 Mini.
  • A gentle tug on each wire leaves it in place.
    Why?
    These wires bend each time the top cover opens. A weak joint would break later.

  2. Plug the USB-C cable into the S2 Mini and into the wall charger. A computer USB socket also works for this test.
    Why?

    Why?
    The control page is where you send commands to the servos.

    Why?
    This screen moves one servo at a time to an exact angle. The normal pose buttons move all eight together.

    Why?
    Servo wires are brown for ground, red for 5 V and orange for signal. Brown on G puts each wire on its matching row. The red wire sits in the middle of the plug, so a plug turned round usually does no harm: the servo stays still until you turn it the right way.

    The bare shaft is small and hard to watch. A horn makes every movement easy to see.

    Why?
    Moving both ways proves the servo can turn in each direction. Holding still between moves proves it keeps an angle. 90° is the middle of its range, a safe place to leave it.

    Why?
    Using one column for every servo means only the servo changes between tests. A servo that fails on a working column is faulty. The dot stops you testing the same servo twice.
  10. Prove the other columns. Move one servo plug to column 1 and move slider S1 R2. Continue through to column 7.
    Why?
    Now the servo stays the same and the column changes. A column that fails has a wiring fault. Testing this way points to the exact cause of any fault.

    Why?
    The horns are needed in Step 19. The shafts stay bare until the joints are fitted at a known angle in Step 29.

  • The display shows a face.
    Why?
    The display and its four wires work.
  • All 10 servos turned and held. At least 8 are needed.
    Why?
    The robot needs eight servos. Two spares cover a faulty servo now or a damaged one later.
  • All 8 columns moved a servo.
    Why?
    Every column and its signal wire work, so any later problem lies somewhere else.

The screw rule

Self-tapping screws cut their own thread in the plastic. Turn slowly and stop as soon as the head sits snug. Extra turns strip the thread.

Fit horns into the hip joints

20 minutes

You need

  • hip joints R1, R2, L1 and L2

Why this step. The horn is the toothed link between a servo shaft and a printed part. Each hip joint links to two servos: the body servo and the leg servo.

Do

  1. Lay out the four hip joints. Take eight single-arm horns from the servo bags. Leave the small screws in the bags.
    Why?
    Each hip joint takes two horns, so eight in total. The joints are fixed to the shafts with M2.5 screws in Steps 29 and 30, so the small screws in the bags stay unused. Leaving them bagged stops them mixing with the M2 screws.
  2. Look at a horn from the side. One side of its round hub stands taller and holds a toothed socket. The other side is shorter.
    Why?
    The hub has a front and a back. Its toothed socket must face outwards, ready for a servo shaft to push into it later.
  3. Find the horn-shaped pockets in a hip joint. Expect two in each part: one for the body servo and one for the leg servo.
    Why?
    Each pocket is the exact shape of a horn, so the horn and the printed part turn together as one piece.
  4. Press a horn into a pocket, shorter side first, until its arm lies flush. The toothed socket faces out.
    Why?
    Shorter side first puts the toothed socket on the outside, where the shaft will enter. Flush means fully seated, with no gap that would let it wobble.
  5. Hold the horn flat. Drive one M2 self-tapping screw through the second hole of the horn arm, counting from the hub, into the plastic. Stop when it is snug.
    Why?
    The pocket stops the horn turning. The screw stops it lifting out when the leg pulls on it. The screw hole in the pocket lines up with that hole of the arm.
  6. Fill every horn pocket on all four hip joints in the same way.
    Why?
    Every servo connects to its joint through a horn.
  7. Keep the hip joints away from the servos for now. The servo shafts stay bare until Step 29.
    Why?
    A joint pushed onto a shaft now would sit at a random angle. Step 29 fits each joint while its servo holds a known angle.

Check before you move on

  • Every pocket holds a horn with its socket facing out.
    Why?
    A horn fitted the wrong way round leaves no socket for the shaft to enter.
  • No horn rocks when you press on its hub.
    Why?
    A loose horn lets the joint wobble, and the robot walks unevenly.
  • You can see daylight through each centre hole.
    Why?
    The M2.5 screw passes through that hole into the servo shaft. A blocked hole means a horn sits wrongly or plastic is in the way.

Wire the battery lead, switch and buck converter input

40 minutes

You need

  • the buck converter
  • red 22 AWG wire
  • heat-shrink
  • iron and solder
  • multimeter

Why this step. The battery's positive wire passes through the switch, so the switch turns the whole robot on and off. Both battery wires then enter the buck converter.

Do

  1. Confirm the battery is unclipped from the pigtail.
    Why?
    Soldering on a circuit that has power risks a short and a burn.
  2. Find the four pads on the buck converter. They are printed IN+, IN-, OUT+ and OUT-.
    Why?
    IN takes power from the battery. OUT gives the lowered voltage. + is positive and - is negative. Mixing them up can destroy the converter.
  3. Tin the two switch terminals, the IN+ pad and the IN- pad.
    Why?
    Parts that already carry solder join with a quick touch of the iron. Less heat keeps the switch's plastic body and the converter's small parts safe.
  4. Joint 1. Slide heat-shrink onto the pigtail's red wire. Solder the red wire to one switch terminal. Shrink the sleeve over the joint.
    Why?
    The switch sits in the positive wire. Opening it breaks the circuit, so everything after it switches off. The sleeve covers the bare metal so it cannot touch anything.
  5. Cut a new piece of red 22 AWG wire long enough to reach from the switch to the buck converter's resting place in the bay, with 3 cm of slack. Strip and tin both ends.
    Why?
    The slack lets you lift the cover and move the converter during later steps without pulling on joints.
  6. Joint 2. Slide heat-shrink onto the new wire and solder one end to the other switch terminal. Shrink the sleeve.
    Why?
    The second terminal is the switched side. Current goes in one terminal and out of the other while the switch is on.
  7. Joint 3. Solder the other end of the new red wire to the pad printed IN+.
    Why?
    This carries battery positive, through the switch, into the converter.
  8. Joint 4. Solder the pigtail's black wire to the pad printed IN-.
    Why?
    The black wire completes the loop back to the battery. One switch in one wire is enough to stop the current, so black runs straight to the converter.
  9. If a pigtail wire is short, lengthen it with 22 AWG wire of the same colour, joined and sleeved as in Step 11.
    Why?
    A wire that barely reaches pulls on its joints. The same colour keeps its meaning clear.
  10. Keep the iron tip clear of the printed parts. PLA softens at soldering temperatures.
    Why?

You need

  • the charged battery
  • multimeter
  • small flat screwdriver

Why this step. A new buck converter arrives set to an unknown voltage. The servos and the S2 Mini need 5 V. You set it now, before its output is connected to anything.

Do

  1. Rest the buck converter on the mat with nothing touching its OUT pads.
    Why?
    Nothing may receive the output until it reads 5.1 V.
  2. Set the rocker switch to O, which is off.
    Why?
    With the switch off, power arrives only when you choose.
  3. Clip the battery onto the pigtail socket.
    Why?
    The converter needs power coming in before there is an output to measure.
  4. Set the multimeter to DC voltage, range 20.
    Why?
    The output could be anywhere up to the battery's 8.4 V at first. The 20 range covers all of it.
  5. Set the rocker switch to I, which is on.
    Why?
    This feeds the battery's power into the converter.
  6. Hold the red probe on the OUT+ pad and the black probe on the OUT- pad. Read the display.
    Why?
    This measures the exact voltage the servos and the S2 Mini will receive.
  7. Turn the small trim screw on the buck converter a little at a time while you watch the reading. If the number moves away from 5.1, turn the other way.
    Why?
    The trim screw turns a tiny adjustable resistor that sets the output. Some need many turns before the reading moves. Small turns keep you from overshooting.
  8. Stop when the reading is 5.10. Any value from 5.05 to 5.15 is fine.
    Why?
    The servos and the S2 Mini are made for 5 V. A little over 5 allows for the small drop along the wires when the servos draw current. Much above 6 V can damage the servos and the board.
  9. Switch off. Unclip the battery.
    Why?
    The output side is wired next, and that needs the power off.

  • The last reading was between 5.05 and 5.15, with no minus sign.
    Why?
    This is the voltage every part in the robot will receive.
  • The battery is unclipped.
    Why?
    The next step solders to the converter, which must have no power.

Connect the buck converter to the rails

30 minutes

You need

  • 22 AWG wire, red and black
  • iron and solder
  • Kapton or electrical tape
  • multimeter
  • the charged battery

Why this step. The 5.1 V output now joins the two rails, so the battery can feed the servos and the S2 Mini.

Do

  1. Confirm the battery is unclipped and the USB cable is unplugged.
    Why?
    You are about to solder the wires that join the battery side to the rails. Both power sources must be off.
  2. Cut one red and one black piece of 22 AWG wire, each long enough to reach from the buck converter's resting place to the header block with 2 cm of slack. Strip and tin all four ends.
    Why?
    Short, direct wires lose less voltage. A little slack lets you lift the header block later.
  3. Solder the red wire to the OUT+ pad and the black wire to the OUT- pad.
    Why?
    These two wires carry the 5.1 V output to the rails.
  4. Lift the header block enough to reach its underside. Solder the black wire to the column 7 joint of the G row. Solder the red wire to the column 7 joint of the + row.
    Why?
    The column 0 end already has the tails to the S2 Mini, so column 7 is the free end. Black goes to ground and red to the 5 V rail, matching their colours.
  5. Repeat the continuity checks from Step 14. Any + pin to any G pin stays silent, or gives one short chirp that stops.
    Why?
    New joints could have joined + to G. The chirp that stops is the capacitors in the converter and the S2 Mini filling up. A beep that keeps going is a short.
  6. Wrap the buck converter in Kapton or electrical tape until no bare metal shows.
    Why?
    The converter has bare metal on both sides. Inside a packed body it could touch pins or wires. Kapton tape copes with the heat a working converter gives off.
  7. Seat the header block again and tuck the buck converter into free space in the bay.
    Why?
    A loose converter rattles and strains its wires as the robot walks.
  8. Test. Clip on the battery and set the switch to I. The display shows the face.
    Why?
    This is the first time the battery powers the robot. The face proves power flows through the switch, the converter, the rails and into the S2 Mini.
  9. Measure on the header block: red probe on any + pin, black probe on any G pin. The reading is 5.05 to 5.15.
    Why?
    This confirms the servos will receive the right voltage at their plugs.
  10. Switch off.
    Why?
    The next step starts from a known state, with the power off.

Check before you move on

  • The display lit up on battery power.
    Why?
    The whole battery path works.
  • The header block reads 5.05 to 5.15 between a + pin and a G pin.
    Why?
    The servo plugs will receive 5.1 V.
  • The buck converter is fully wrapped.
    Why?
    No bare metal is left to touch anything.

Calibrate and close up

The servos are powered first and told to hold a known pose. The joints go on afterwards, at that known angle. This order is what makes the robot stand straight.

Why this step. With the power on and the Stand pose selected, each servo turns to its Stand angle the moment you plug it in.

Safety

Why?
A servo turns only 180°. A joint fitted at a random angle can hit the body as the servo turns, and the motor keeps pushing, which strains its gears and motor.

    USB and the battery feed the same 5 V rail through VBUS. With both on, two supplies push into each other, and current can flow back into the computer or the converter. A wall charger holds a steady 5 V, while a battery's voltage falls as it empties.
  2. Rest the body on an upturned mug so its four corners hang free.
    Why?
    The legs can then turn in the air without hitting the table, and the robot cannot walk away.
  3. Join the Sesame-Controller Wi-Fi on your phone and open the control page.
    Why?
    The control page sends the pose commands.

    Why?

    Why?
    Each column carries one servo's instructions. The whirr is the servo turning to its Stand angle.

    Why?
    One at a time, you see each servo respond, and a wrong one shows up at once. Leg servos lie loose because their legs are not fitted yet.
  7. Tap Rest, then Stand. All eight shafts turn and stop together.
    Why?
    This proves all eight servos follow commands before any joint is attached.

    Why?
    Right column and right way round means each servo gets its own instructions.
  • All eight shafts move when you tap Rest and Stand.
    Why?
    Every servo responds to the program.

Attach the four hip joints

30 minutes

You need

  • the four hip joints with their horns
  • 4 M2.5 machine screws
  • cross-head screwdriver
  • your phone

Why this step. Each hip joint goes on while its servo holds the Stand angle, so Stand always means the same shape.

Do

  1. Tap Stand and leave the robot holding that pose.
    Why?
    Each joint is fitted while its servo holds the Stand angle. That ties the servo's angle to the joint's position, so Stand always gives the same shape.
  2. Take hip joint R1. Its place is the front right corner, on servo S0.
    Why?
    R1 is the front right hip, driven by S0.
  3. Lower its horn onto the servo shaft so that the hip joint points away from the centre of the body at 45°: forward and to the right. The teeth allow positions about 17° apart. Choose the position that lands on 45° or right beside it.
    Why?
    In Stand the four hips form an X, each at 45° from the side of the body. The teeth allow steps of about 17° only, so you pick the closest step.
  4. Tap Rest. The hip joint swings to point straight out to the right, square to the side of the body.
    Why?
    Rest is 45° away from Stand. Seeing both poses land correctly confirms the joint is on right.
  5. Tap Stand. It returns to 45°.
    Why?
    Returning to 45° confirms the joint moves as the program expects.
  6. If either pose looks more than a few degrees off, lift the joint off, turn it by one tooth and push it back on.
    Why?
    One tooth is one step of about 17°. A joint more than a few degrees off makes the robot lean.
  7. Fix it. Drive one M2.5 machine screw through the centre hole into the servo shaft. Hold the hip joint still with your other hand while you turn. Stop when it is snug.
    Why?
    The servo shaft has a threaded hole for this screw. The screw stops the joint pulling off. Holding the joint still keeps the screw from turning the shaft and straining the servo's gears. Overtightening can strip the thread in the shaft.
  8. Repeat for R2 on S1 (back right: it points backward and right), L1 on S2 (front left: forward and left) and L2 on S3 (back left: backward and left).
    Why?
    Each hip points diagonally away from its corner at Stand.
  9. Tap Rest and Stand once more and compare both poses with the diagram.
    Why?
    A final look catches a joint that shifted while you screwed it.

Check before you move on

  • At Stand the four hip joints form an X.
    Why?
    An X at Stand means all four hips are at their correct angles.
  • At Rest all four point straight out sideways, two on each side.
    Why?
    Straight out at Rest confirms each hip swings the full 45°.
  • Four M2.5 screws are in and snug.
    Why?
    Without its screw a joint can work loose while walking.

Do

    Why?
    You will press on wires and fit screws. With the power off, a slip cannot short anything.

    Why?
    The channels are grooves printed for the leads. Leads pressed in stay clear of the moving legs.

    Why?
    The battery now lives in its cavity, connected to the robot.
  9. Fit the bottom cover with every wire inside. Fix it with two M2 self-tapping screws.

    The cover holds the battery in place.

    An edge that sits high means a wire is trapped under it.

    This finds wires that would snag while walking. With the power on, a servo resists your hand, and forcing it strains its gears.

    You need the switch every day and the port for future uploads.

Add a voice and connect Syd

This part gives the robot a microphone, a speaker and a link to Syd. You say a wake word and then a question. The robot answers aloud with Syd's reply while its face follows the conversation. Your hands stay free the whole time.

Why?
The voice module streams sound to the laptop all the time. The laptop listens for the wake word only and lets everything else pass. Once it hears the wake word, it records one sentence.

Three devices work together. The voice module is a small board with a microphone and a speaker that rides on the robot. The laptop runs a program called the relay. The robot shows faces and makes movements. Wake word detection and speech processing need more computing power than the two small boards have, so the laptop does that work. The robot can talk whenever the relay is running on the laptop.

Why?
Wake word detection and speech recognition use trained models that need far more memory and computing power than the two small boards have. The laptop has enough. Splitting the work this way leaves the robot's own program almost unchanged.

The programs in this part were written for this handbook. The relay ran complete conversations with a simulated voice module, using stand-ins for the wake word and speech libraries. The two sketches for the voice module were compiled and run on a simulated board. They have yet to run on a real Atom EchoS3R, so allow time for small adjustments. // Voice module self-test for the M5Stack Atom EchoS3R.
// It beeps, records three seconds, plays the recording back, then repeats.
// No Wi-Fi is used. This proves the speaker and the microphone work.

#include <M5Unified.h>

static const uint32_t RATE = 16000;        // samples per second
static const size_t CHUNK = 1600;          // a tenth of a second
static const size_t CHUNKS = 30;           // three seconds in total
static int16_t* recording = nullptr;

void setup() {
  M5.begin();
  Serial.begin(115200);
  recording = (int16_t*)heap_caps_malloc(CHUNK * CHUNKS * sizeof(int16_t), MALLOC_CAP_8BIT);
  if (recording == nullptr) {
    Serial.println("Out of memory. Check that the board is set to M5AtomS3R.");
    while (true) { delay(1000); }
  }
  memset(recording, 0, CHUNK * CHUNKS * sizeof(int16_t));
}

void loop() {
  // 1. Beep through the speaker.
  M5.Speaker.begin();
  M5.Speaker.setVolume(180);
  M5.Speaker.tone(1000, 200);
  while (M5.Speaker.isPlaying()) { delay(1); }

  // 2. Record. The microphone and the speaker share one audio chip, so the speaker stops first.
  M5.Speaker.end();
  M5.Mic.begin();
  Serial.println("Recording for 3 seconds. Speak now.");
  for (size_t i = 0; i < CHUNKS; i++) {
    M5.Mic.record(&recording[i * CHUNK], CHUNK, RATE);
  }
  while (M5.Mic.isRecording()) { delay(1); }

  int32_t loudest = 0;
  for (size_t i = 0; i < CHUNK * CHUNKS; i++) {
    int32_t level = abs((int32_t)recording[i]);
    if (level > loudest) { loudest = level; }
  }
  Serial.printf("Loudest sample: %d of 32767\n", (int)loudest);

  // 3. Play the recording back.
  M5.Mic.end();
  M5.Speaker.begin();
  M5.Speaker.setVolume(180);
  Serial.println("Playing it back.");
  M5.Speaker.playRaw(recording, CHUNK * CHUNKS, RATE, false, 1, 0);
  while (M5.Speaker.isPlaying()) { delay(1); }
  delay(2000);
}

class Turn:
    """Collects one sentence and decides when the speaker has finished."""

    def __init__(self, vad, wait_ms, settle_ms=0):
        self.vad = vad
        self.wait_ms = wait_ms              # None means wait for ever
        self.settle_ms = settle_ms          # speech in this first stretch does not start a sentence
        self.before = collections.deque(maxlen=8)
        self.audio = bytearray()
        self.started = False
        self.run_ms = 0
        self.waited_ms = 0
        self.silence_ms = 0
        self.length_ms = 0

    def add(self, frame):
        """Returns "more", "done" or "nothing"."""
        voiced = sum(
            self.vad.is_speech(frame[i:i + SLICE_BYTES], RATE)
            for i in range(0, FRAME_BYTES, SLICE_BYTES)
        )
        speaking = voiced >= 2
        if not self.started:
            self.before.append(frame)
            if self.settle_ms > 0:
                self.settle_ms -= 80
                return "more"
            self.run_ms = self.run_ms + 80 if speaking else 0
            if self.run_ms >= S.MIN_SPEECH_MS:
                self.started = True
                self.audio = bytearray(b"".join(self.before))
                self.length_ms = len(self.audio) // 32
                return "more"
            self.waited_ms += 80
            if self.wait_ms is not None and self.waited_ms >= self.wait_ms:
                return "nothing"
            return "more"
        self.audio += frame
        self.length_ms += 80
        self.silence_ms = 0 if speaking else self.silence_ms + 80
        if self.silence_ms >= S.END_SILENCE_MS or self.length_ms >= S.MAX_TURN_MS:
            extra_ms = self.silence_ms - KEEP_SILENCE_MS      # trim most of the quiet at the end
            if extra_ms > 0:
                del self.audio[len(self.audio) - extra_ms * 32:]
            return "done"
        return "more"

async def speak(writer, pcm):
    """Play speech through the module at the speed it is spoken."""
    if len(pcm) % 2:
        pcm = pcm[:-1]
    writer.write(pack("S"))
    await writer.drain()
    start = time.monotonic()
    sent = 0
    for i in range(0, len(pcm), PLAY_CHUNK):
        chunk = pcm[i:i + PLAY_CHUNK]
        writer.write(pack("A", chunk))
        await writer.drain()
        sent += len(chunk)
        ahead = sent / (RATE * 2) - (time.monotonic() - start)
        if ahead > LEAD_S:
            await asyncio.sleep(ahead - LEAD_S)
    left = len(pcm) / (RATE * 2) - (time.monotonic() - start)
    if left > 0:

        self.writer = writer

            self.wake.reset()
        self.quiet_until = time.monotonic() + 1.0
        robot_later(face=S.FACE_WAITING)
        if S.USE_WAKE_WORD:
            self.state = "waiting"
            say("Waiting for the wake word.")
        else:
            self.turn = Turn(self.vad, None)
            self.state = "listening"
            say("Listening. Speak whenever you like.")

    def show_level(self, frame):
        now = time.monotonic()
        if S.SHOW_LEVEL and now >= self.level_at:
            self.level_at = now + 2
            peak = int(np.abs(np.frombuffer(frame, dtype=np.int16).astype(np.int32)).max())
            say("Mic level %d%%" % round(peak * 100 / 32768))

    async def run(self):
        while True:
            kind, data = await read_message(self.reader)
            if kind == "H":
                say("Module says hello:", data.decode(errors="replace"))
            elif kind == "R":
                if self.state == "wait_ready":
                    if self.after_ready == "followup" and S.FOLLOWUP_MS > 0:
                        self.turn = Turn(self.vad, S.FOLLOWUP_MS)
                        self.state = "listening"
                        robot_later(face=S.FACE_FOLLOWUP)
                        say("Listening for a follow-up.")
                    else:
                        self.wait_for_wake_word()
                    self.after_ready = "waiting"
            elif kind == "A" and len(data) == FRAME_BYTES:
                if S.MIC_GAIN != 1.0:
                    louder = np.frombuffer(data, dtype=np.int16).astype(np.float32) * S.MIC_GAIN
                    data = np.clip(louder, -32768, 32767).astype(np.int16).tobytes()
                await self.hear(data)

    async def hear(self, frame):
        if self.state == "waiting":
            self.show_level(frame)
            score = self.wake.score(frame)
            if score >= S.WAKE_THRESHOLD and time.monotonic() >= self.quiet_until:
                say("Wake word heard (score %.2f). Listening." % score)
                robot_later(face=S.FACE_WAKE)
                self.turn = Turn(self.vad, S.WAKE_WAIT_MS, S.WAKE_SETTLE_MS)
                self.state = "listening"
        elif self.state == "listening":
            result = self.turn.add(frame)
            if result == "nothing":
                say("Heard no speech.")
                self.wait_for_wake_word()
            elif result == "done":
                pcm = bytes(self.turn.audio)
                say("Heard %.1f s of speech. Thinking." % (len(pcm) / (RATE * 2)))
                if S.SAVE_LAST_WAV:
                    save_wav("last_heard.wav", pcm)
                robot_later(face=S.FACE_THINKING)
                self.thinking = asyncio.create_task(asyncio.to_thread(brain, pcm))
                self.state = "thinking"
        elif self.state == "thinking" and self.thinking.done():
            await self.answer()

    async def answer(self):
        try:
            text, speech = self.thinking.result()
        except Exception as error:
            say("The brain failed:", repr(error))
            robot_later(face=S.FACE_PROBLEM)
            await asyncio.sleep(2)
            self.wait_for_wake_word()
            return
        if not speech:
            say("Nothing to say back.")
            self.wait_for_wake_word()
            return
        say("Reply:", text)
        say("Speaking for %.1f s." % (len(speech) / (RATE * 2)))
        command, face = expression_for(text)
        robot_later(command=command, face=face)
        self.state = "wait_ready"
        self.after_ready = "followup"
        await speak(self.writer, speech)

def to_16k(samples, rate):
    """Resample mono 16-bit samples from any rate to 16 kHz. Returns raw speech bytes."""
    x = np.asarray(samples, dtype=np.float32)
    if rate != RATE and len(x) > 1:
        if rate > RATE:
            taps = 63
            cutoff = 0.45 * RATE / rate
            n = np.arange(taps) - (taps - 1) / 2
            low_pass = 2 * cutoff * np.sinc(2 * cutoff * n) * np.hamming(taps)
            x = np.convolve(x, low_pass / low_pass.sum(), mode="same")
        positions = np.arange(0, len(x) - 1, rate / RATE)
        x = np.interp(positions, np.arange(len(x)), x)
    return np.clip(np.round(x), -32768, 32767).astype(np.int16).tobytes()


def pcm_to_wav(pcm, path=None):
    """Wrap raw speech as a WAV file. Returns the bytes, and saves them if you give a path."""
    buffer = io.BytesIO()
    with wave.open(buffer, "wb") as out:
        out.setnchannels(1)
        out.setsampwidth(2)
        out.setframerate(RATE)
        out.writeframes(pcm)
    data = buffer.getvalue()
    if path:
        pathlib.Path(path).write_bytes(data)
    return data


def audio_to_pcm(data):
    """Convert an audio file's bytes to raw speech. WAV works as it is. Other formats (mp3, ogg) need ffmpeg."""
    if data[:4] == b"RIFF":
        with wave.open(io.BytesIO(data)) as source:
            if source.getsampwidth() == 2:
                samples = np.frombuffer(source.readframes(source.getnframes()), dtype=np.int16)
                channels = source.getnchannels()
                if channels > 1:
                    samples = samples.reshape(-1, channels).mean(axis=1)
                return to_16k(samples, source.getframerate())
    done = subprocess.run(
        ["ffmpeg", "-loglevel", "error", "-i", "pipe:0", "-f", "s16le", "-ac", "1", "-ar", str(RATE), "pipe:1"],
        input=data, stdout=subprocess.PIPE, check=True,
    )
    return done.stdout

def tidy(text):
    """Remove what should stay unspoken: markdown symbols, links and emoji. Trim a very long reply."""
    text = re.sub(r"\[([^\]]+)\]\([^)]+\)", r"\1", text)             # [label](link) becomes label
    text = re.sub(r"https?://\S+", "", text)                          # bare links
    text = re.sub(r"^\s*[-\u2022]\s+", "", text, flags=re.M)          # list bullets
    text = re.sub(r"[*_#`>|~]+", " ", text)                           # markdown symbols
    text = re.sub("[\U0001F000-\U0001FAFF\u2600-\u27BF\uFE0F\u200D]", "", text)   # emoji
    text = " ".join(text.split())

        stop = max(cut.rfind(". "), cut.rfind("? "), cut.rfind("! "))

void sendMsg(char kind, const uint8_t* data, uint16_t len) {
  uint8_t head[3] = { (uint8_t)kind, (uint8_t)(len >> 8), (uint8_t)(len & 0xFF) };
  relay.write(head, 3);
  if (len > 0) { relay.write(data, len); }

bool readExact(uint8_t* dst, size_t count) {
  size_t got = 0;
  uint32_t lastData = millis();
  while (got < count) {
    int n = relay.read(dst + got, count - got);
    if (n > 0) {
      got += n;
      lastData = millis();
    } else if (!relay.connected() || millis() - lastData > 3000) {
      return false;
    } else {
      delay(1);
    }
  }
  return true;
}

      if (!readExact((uint8_t*)playBuf[playIdx], len)) { return; }
      if (speaking) {
        M5.Speaker.playRaw(playBuf[playIdx], len / 2, RATE, false, 1, 0);
      }
      playIdx = (playIdx + 1) % 3;
    } else {
      uint8_t skip[64];
      while (len > 0) {
        size_t part = len > sizeof(skip) ? sizeof(skip) : len;
        if (!readExact(skip, part)) { return; }
        len -= part;
      }
    }
  }
}

// Record one frame and send the frame that finished two calls ago.
void pumpMic() {
  if (M5.Mic.record(micBuf[micIdx], MIC_SAMPLES, RATE)) {
    if (micPrimed >= 2) {
      uint8_t ready = (micIdx + 2) % 4;
      sendMsg('A', (const uint8_t*)micBuf[ready], MIC_SAMPLES * 2);
    } else {
      micPrimed++;
    }
    micIdx = (micIdx + 1) % 4;
  } else {
    delay(5);
  }
}

bool findRelay() {
  if (strlen(RELAY_HOST) > 0) {
    return WiFi.hostByName(RELAY_HOST, relayIp) == 1;
  }
  if (beacon.parsePacket() <= 0) { return false; }
  char text[32];
  int n = beacon.read(text, sizeof(text) - 1);
  if (n <= 0) { return false; }
  text[n] = 0;
  if (strncmp(text, "SYD-RELAY", 9) != 0) { return false; }
  relayIp = beacon.remoteIP();
  return true;
}

void setup() {
  M5.begin();

  beep(880);

}

void loop() {
  if (!relay.connected()) {
    relay.stop();
    if (!findRelay()) {
      delay(200);
      return;
    }
    Serial.print("Relay found at ");
    Serial.println(relayIp);
    if (!relay.connect(relayIp, RELAY_PORT)) {
      Serial.println("The relay did not accept the connection. Trying again.");
      delay(1000);
      return;
    }
    relay.setNoDelay(true);
    Serial.println("Connected to the relay.");
    beep(1320);
    sendMsg('H', (const uint8_t*)"syd-voice 1", 11);
    startListening();
    return;
  }
  handleIncoming();
  if (speaking) {
    delay(2);
  } else {
    pumpMic();
  }
}

Train your own wake word (optional)

3 hours, mostly waiting

You need

  • a Google account
  • the Chrome browser
  • the laptop

Why this step. "Hey Jarvis" is a ready-made model. Training your own lets the robot answer to a phrase you choose, such as "Hey Syd". The ready-made models are licensed for non-commercial use, so train your own for anything beyond personal use.

Do

  1. Open the training notebook in Chrome and sign in with your Google account.
    Why?
    The notebook runs on Google's computers, which have the graphics processors that training needs.
  2. In the first form, set TARGET_PHRASE to hey sid. Spell the phrase the way it sounds, in lower case. Set MODEL_NAME to hey_syd.
    Why?
    The notebook makes thousands of computer-voiced clips of this phrase to learn from. Spelling it as it sounds gives the right pronunciation.
  3. Choose Runtime, then Change runtime type. Pick T4 GPU and click Save.
    Why?
    A graphics processor trains the model many times faster than a normal processor.
  4. Choose Runtime, then Run all. Accept the notice that the notebook comes from GitHub. Training takes about 2.5 hours on the free T4 GPU. Keep the browser tab open.
    Why?
    Each part of the notebook does one stage: make voice clips, add background noise, train the model, save it as a file.
  5. When the last cell finishes, open the Files panel on the left side of the notebook, find hey_syd.onnx and download it.
    Why?
    This single file is your trained wake word model.
  6. Move hey_syd.onnx into the syd-robot folder.
    Why?
    The relay looks for the model in its own folder.
  7. Open settings.py and change the wake word line to WAKE_MODEL = "hey_syd.onnx". Save the file and restart the relay.
    Why?
    This line tells the relay to use your model in place of "Hey Jarvis".
  8. Say Hey Syd ten times from different places in the room. Count how often the relay hears it.
    Why?
    A count gives a hit rate you can compare after each change.
  9. Leave a radio or a video playing for 30 minutes. Count how often the relay wakes with nobody saying the phrase. Adjust WAKE_THRESHOLD as in Step 37.
    Why?
    False wakes usually happen during background speech. Testing with it shows how the robot behaves in a lived-in room.

Reference: the openWakeWord project.

Check before you move on

  • The relay prints Wake word model loaded: hey_syd.onnx.
    Why?
    The relay loaded your model.
  • The robot woke to your phrase at least eight times in ten.
    Why?
    Eight in ten or better is reliable for daily use.
  • The robot stayed quiet through most of the 30 minutes of background speech.
    Why?
    Few false wakes means the robot stays quiet until called.

Daily use

  1. Laptop: open a terminal in the project folder and start the relay. Wait for the Relay ready line.
    Why?
    The module looks for the relay as soon as it starts, so the relay goes first.
  2. Robot: set the switch to I. Listen for one low beep, then one high beep.
    Why?
    The low beep means power. The high beep means the module found the relay.
  3. Say the wake word, wait for the excited face, then speak.
    Why?
    The excited face shows the relay heard the wake word and is recording.
  4. Wait for the happy face before a follow-up question.
    Why?
    The happy face shows the microphone is back on after the reply.
  5. To finish: tap Rest on the phone, set the switch to O and press Ctrl+C in the relay window.
    Why?
    Rest lowers the robot before the power goes. Ctrl+C stops the relay cleanly.

To load a new program onto the voice module later, pull it off its pad, unplug the power lead from it, then connect the USB cable.

Why?
The module may have one power source at a time.
